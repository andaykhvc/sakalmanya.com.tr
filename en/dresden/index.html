City portrait · Saxony

Dresden

A city where Baroque stone, scorched memory and silicon face one another on the same riverbank.

Photo: Michele Vascellari · CC BY-SA 4.0 · Open source ↗

Dresden first presents itself like a postcard: the Elbe moves at an unhurried pace, domes gather the evening light, and stone façades carry themselves as though history had never become coarse. Yet this city is not merely beautiful. It is the result of a long negotiation between beauty and power, destruction and memory, science and capital.

Augustus the Strong: when splendour was political stagecraft

To understand Dresden's present silhouette, we need to return to 1694, when Frederick Augustus I of Saxony, better known as Augustus the Strong, came to power. Authority is visible not only in armies and treasuries but in the way a stage is arranged. Augustus understood that perfectly. Court, collections, architecture and ceremony were the silk gloves of state power. Under his rule, an intense building campaign transformed Dresden into the “Pearl of the Elbe”, while its art collections grew into some of the richest in Europe.

The Golden Rider still stands on Neustädter Markt, clad in Roman armour and gold leaf, looking towards Poland. Augustus had secured the Polish crown in 1697. The monument therefore tells us more than the story of one ruler: it suggests that Dresden's aesthetic was not an accident but a deliberate composition of power.

A city's beauty cannot conceal crime and suffering

Dresden did not stand outside the Nazi dictatorship after 1933. Jews, Communists and Social Democrats were removed from public office; during the pogroms of 1938, the synagogue designed by Gottfried Semper was set on fire. The city was woven into the economic and transport system of the war started by Germany. It was not simply a victim at the end, but first part of a functioning geography of persecution. Reading history honestly does not mean ranking whose pain fits more neatly into an elegant sentence.

Between 13 and 15 February 1945, four attacks by British and American air forces turned the city centre and extensive residential areas into a firestorm. Historical research now places the death toll at approximately 25,000. The appalling deaths of civilians, the loss of homes and the destruction of cultural heritage must be named without hesitation. Yet the legally disputed label of “war crime” should not be used as an easy slogan at the expense of context: Nazi persecution continued in the same city, the war economy remained active, and Europe had spent six years paying the price of Germany's war of annihilation.

Dresden's moral weight does not lie in a single night. It lies in the unsettling distance between Baroque magnificence and dictatorship, civilian destruction and political responsibility.

Soviet occupation, the GDR and the second life of stone

The Red Army reached Dresden in May 1945, and the Soviet Military Administration took control. The new order ended National Socialism but did not resolve itself into a simple story of freedom: political purges, forced mergers, a new one-party government and another authoritarian state came to shape daily life. Following the establishment of the GDR in 1949, Dresden became a district capital. Strikes and demonstrations also took place here during the uprising of 17 June 1953.

Even so, the city was never reduced entirely to prefabricated concrete and the planned economy. The Zwinger, Semperoper and other historic buildings rose again piece by piece; the dark stones of the Frauenkirche remained open to the sky for decades like a war memorial. In 1989, Dresden residents took part in the mass demonstrations of the Peaceful Revolution. The city's post-reunification story is therefore not only about restoring old stone but also about building a new economic identity.

Two institutions, two distinct educational characters

Dresden does not offer students a single academic route. On one side are the expansive laboratories of a research university; on the other, the more immediate professional and industrial links of a university of applied sciences. Instead of asking which is “better”, it is wiser to ask how you want to learn.

Ausbildung: clean rooms, workbenches and real professions

Dresden's educational landscape does not end at the university gate. The city offers dual vocational routes for electronics technicians, mechatronics technicians, microtechnologists, IT specialists and other industrial technical professions. Careers are built not only in lecture halls, but also on production lines, at vocational schools and in corporate laboratories. The BSZ Elektrotechnik is an important training centre for technical occupations in the microelectronics sector.

In 2025, GlobalFoundries, Infineon, Bosch and X-FAB recruited a record number of new apprentices, while ESMC launched its first mechatronics and microtechnology cohorts. According to an industry forecast cited by the City of Dresden, Saxony's high-tech sector may need at least 27,000 additional workers by 2030. This is not a job guarantee. It does, however, reveal where the region is directing its money, schools and workforce.

From Baroque domes to wafers: Dresden's affair with electronics

Dresden is now one of Europe's largest microelectronics centres. According to the city, one in every three chips made in Europe is produced in Dresden, while the Silicon Saxony network connects hundreds of companies and research institutions. GlobalFoundries, Bosch, Infineon and X-FAB are now being joined by an even larger name: TSMC.

ESMC, led by TSMC with a 70 percent share and joined by Bosch, Infineon and NXP with 10 percent each, is building an advanced semiconductor fab in Dresden for automotive and industrial applications. Official plans place the investment above €10 billion. Production is targeted for late 2027, with a planned capacity of 40,000 300 mm wafers per month and around 2,000 direct high-tech jobs at full operation. These numbers do not represent engineering desks alone: technicians, maintenance, quality, logistics, chemistry, IT and production form the invisible backbone of such a facility.

Dresden is no longer merely a city of porcelain cups, opera boxes and stone domes. Here, the material of the future begins as sand, and precision is measured in nanometres.

Who is Dresden right for?

Dresden suits students who prefer a dense cultural and technological environment to the constant noise of a major metropolis. History and art sit on the doorstep, Saxon Switzerland is nearby, and university study, applied education, Ausbildung and high-tech companies meet on the same urban map. Yet the city should not be chosen solely through romantic photographs of the Altstadt. The fractures of eastern Germany's transformation, present-day political tensions and the everyday importance of German for international students also belong in the portrait.

It is best to see Dresden not as a backdrop but as a decision. Your subject, budget, German level and plans after graduation belong on the same table. Dresden may offer both an education and an industrial environment, but no city can take the place of a realistic plan.
