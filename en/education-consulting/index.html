A plan for Germany starts with the right questions

Guidance, to me, is not about firing off an application to a random list of institutions. We start by reading your goal, your school record, your level of German, the time you have and what your family expects. University, Hochschule, Ausbildung and a language year can look alike from the outside, but each one asks for its own calendar, its own paperwork and its own kind of effort.

Living in Dresden as a student taught me this much: romance about Germany is a fine place to start, but it does not carry you. The system speaks in documents, deadlines, levels and proof. So I would rather build a plan you can actually follow than hand you a pleasant promise.

My job in a consultation is not to decide for you. It is to make your options visible, keep you from spending months in the wrong direction, and help you build the architecture of your application properly.

What do we work through together?

For a university or Hochschule application we look at how well the programme fits you, the shortlist of institutions, the deadlines, your diploma and transcript, your language level and your motivation letter. If the goal is an Ausbildung, the focus shifts to the occupational field, employer research, your CV, your German and how you communicate with the company.

On the language side, aiming for B1 within twelve months gives a complete beginner a realistic backbone. Some people move faster; others need more room because of school, work or exams. What matters is steady tracking, measurable homework and genuine speaking practice.

When it comes to documents, we go through what is missing, what needs translating or certifying, and how the CV and motivation letter read. For the visa, we plan the order of the file and the preparation for the appointment — knowing all along that the official decision belongs to the relevant authority.

Who is this for?

Students in their final year of school who are thinking about Germany. People already studying in Turkey who want to explore a move. Anyone trying to understand the difference between a Hochschule and a Universität. Applicants aiming for an Ausbildung. And parents who want to follow what their child is walking into.

I am not an official body, and I do not guarantee admission, a visa or residence. What we can do together is simplify what to look at in each phase, when each document needs to be ready, and which goal actually looks realistic.

A good plan for Germany is not built out of an elegant sentence about dreams. It is built out of a well-kept file. If you want to take your goal seriously, this work gives you a clear place to start.

What belongs on the table before we talk

Before the first conversation, your school record, graduation status, transcript, current level of German and the field or occupation you are aiming at should all be visible together. Advice given without them tends to float. To draw a route that holds, we first have to read honestly where you are standing.

Here is what I saw while living in Dresden: the German system does not read good intentions, it reads a prepared file. So instead of simply saying “apply”, it is more useful to put the shortlist, the document calendar, the language target, the finances and the family’s expectations on the same table.

A plan is allowed to change along the way. A Hochschule may start to make more sense than a university, an Ausbildung may become the stronger option, or a year of language may need to come first. None of that is failure — it is what reading the process more accurately looks like.

What should you expect from this work?

After a consultation you should walk away with more than a one-line piece of advice — you should have a readable framework that shows which step comes when. It ties together the university, the language, the documents, the dates and the correspondence. That is what pulls the process out of scattered messages and last-minute panic.

No two files look alike. For one applicant the grade average is the strong card, for another the language moves faster, for a third it is the work experience that actually counts towards an Ausbildung. So rather than forcing every application into the same template, it is better to name the strengths and the weak spots honestly.

The best outcomes come when you know your own share of the work. I simplify the route, make the documents and the calendar visible, and explain how the German system thinks. The effort, the follow-through and the final decision stay with you — that is simply part of the process.

That is why every conversation covers not only today's application but also the step after it. Which options narrow if your German stops improving? Which deadline slips if a document arrives late? Which institutions need a second look if the target city changes? Questions like these are what make a plan hold up. Good guidance does not put a single road in front of you — it makes the options and the risks visible.
